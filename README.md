## What marketing agencies actually use proxies for

A proxy routes web requests through an IP address other than the agency’s office or cloud-server address. For marketing work, this matters when websites, search results, ads, offers, or content vary by location, IP reputation, or request volume.

The useful cases tend to fall into a few clear categories.

### Localized SEO and SERP observation

Search results can differ by country, state, language, device, and search history. An agency tracking organic visibility for a US client may want to collect public results from relevant US locations rather than relying solely on what appears from its own network.

This is useful for:

- Monitoring keyword positions across target markets
- Reviewing localized competitor pages and offers
- Comparing search-result layouts over time
- Checking whether a landing page resolves correctly for US visitors
- Collecting public SERP data for reports and internal research

There is an important limit here: Google results are shaped by more than an IP address. Device settings, language, cookies, personalization, and location signals can all affect the page. A proxy is one piece of the observation setup, not a magic “show me exactly what every user sees” button.

### Competitor price and offer monitoring

Retail, travel, SaaS, and subscription clients often change prices, bundles, shipping thresholds, coupon banners, and availability. A static IP pool can help an agency run scheduled checks of public pages without putting every request through a single office connection.

For a US-focused monitoring workflow, stable ISP IPs are often easier to manage than constantly rotating addresses when the same pages need repeated visits. The appeal is less glamorous than some proxy sales pages make it sound: repeatable collection, stable sessions, and no bandwidth meter creeping upward in the background.

### Campaign landing-page QA

Campaign QA often includes checking:

- Redirect behavior by region
- Currency and shipping disclosures
- Consent banners and geo-specific messaging
- Local promotion eligibility
- Page speed and assets as delivered to a visitor
- Whether the paid-ad destination matches the expected page

A proxy can help with public, visitor-side checks. It should not become the default way to access a client’s advertising account or social-media admin panel.

### Ad verification and creative checks

Agencies may need to see public ads, landing pages, or regional promotions from a visitor perspective. In many cases, platform-provided tools should be the first stop: public ad libraries, transparency centers, and account reporting can answer a surprising number of questions without any proxy traffic.

Use proxies when the question is specifically about the public web experience—for example, whether a public destination page, localized offer, or visible creative differs for a target geography. Keep the scope narrow and documented.

### Public competitor and reputation research

Public review pages, competitor blogs, store locators, marketplace listings, and campaign pages can all be valid research inputs. The key is to use reasonable request rates, respect applicable website terms and access controls, and avoid collecting personal data that the client does not actually need.

> A proxy is a research infrastructure tool, not a substitute for proper platform access, client authorization, or a sensible data-collection policy.

## Jobs that usually do **not** need a proxy

The simplest way to save money is to avoid buying proxy capacity for work that has a better route.

### Client ad-account and social-account management

Do not treat a proxy as the standard solution for logging into a client’s Google Ads, Meta, LinkedIn, TikTok, or social account. Shared credentials, unfamiliar devices, unusual locations, and proxy IPs can create security reviews at exactly the wrong time.

Use delegated access instead:

- Google Ads manager-account access
- Meta business partner access
- Role-based access for social pages
- Client-owned credentials and documented permissions
- Revocable agency access rather than shared passwords

This preserves ownership for the client and gives everyone a clearer audit trail.

### Crawling a client website you are authorized to audit

If the agency has permission to crawl a client’s site, an allowlisted agency server or crawler IP is often cleaner than routing the crawl through a rotating or distributed proxy setup. It also makes troubleshooting much less theatrical.

### One-off manual research

If someone needs to check three competitor pages once, buying a 50-IP plan is unlikely to be the elegant answer. Use existing research tools, browser testing services, public ad libraries, or a manual review process first.

## How many proxies does an agency need?

Client count is a poor sizing metric. An agency with 30 clients may need less capacity than an agency with four ecommerce clients if the first agency runs a few daily rank checks and the second performs frequent catalog, price, and stock monitoring.

Start with the workload instead.

### 1. List recurring jobs

For each task, record:

- Client or internal project
- Target country or state
- Number of pages or queries
- Frequency
- Expected page size
- Whether the session must remain stable
- Whether jobs overlap in time
- Whether a first-party tool or API can replace collection

A simple job list quickly reveals whether the agency needs many IPs or just better scheduling.

### 2. Find the busiest overlap window

The important number is not total monthly requests. It is the number of requests running at the same time during the busiest part of the schedule.

For example:

- Rank tracking runs overnight
- Price monitoring runs every four hours
- Ad QA happens during local business hours
- Manual research occurs during the team’s workday

If all of these run simultaneously, capacity needs rise. If they are scheduled apart, the same team may need fewer IPs and less operational complexity.

### 3. Separate stable-session work from broad collection

Use stable static IPs when a task benefits from consistency, such as repeated checks against public pages over a period of time. Avoid assigning the same IP casually to unrelated jobs, clients, and team members. It becomes difficult to diagnose blocks, performance issues, or access mistakes later.

A practical structure is:

- Separate credentials by tool or job family
- Keep an internal client/project tag for each scheduled job
- Document which IP allocation supports which workflow
- Rotate access when staff or clients leave
- Keep proxy credentials out of client-owned systems unless explicitly approved

This is dull operational hygiene. It is also the part that prevents a “small proxy subscription” from becoming an untraceable recurring cost.

## A sensible agency rollout

Do not roll proxy access into every service immediately. Start with one clear and measurable workload.

1. **Pick a public-data use case.** Examples: US price monitoring, landing-page QA, or competitor-offer collection.
2. **Define success before buying.** Track completion rate, response times, blocked requests, data accuracy, and staff time saved.
3. **Use a small operational scope.** Assign a specific team member, tool, schedule, and client list.
4. **Test on actual public target pages.** Demo pages and home-page benchmarks do not reflect your clients’ sites.
5. **Review the cost after one billing cycle.** Check whether the work produced useful client deliverables or simply created more dashboards.
6. **Scale only when capacity is the real constraint.** More IPs do not fix an unclear workflow.
